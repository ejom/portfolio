ASU · Advanced semiconductor packaging research · Summer 2026

              
Full-wave Ansys HFSS modeling of package signal waveguides

              
I built HFSS models of the interconnects that carry high-speed signals through advanced packages: coplanar waveguides and striplines, where a signal trace runs in a dielectric between two ground planes and is fenced by stitching vias. I extracted S-parameters and read them on Smith charts to study impedance matching, insertion and return loss, and how via pitch and fence spacing suppress parasitic plane modes. This fed into studying die-to-die links like Intel EMIB and TSMC CoWoS.

              
  • Ansys HFSS
  • S-parameters
  • Smith charts
  • Stripline / CPW
  • Via fencing
  • EMIB / CoWoS
  • Cleanroom: litho, etch, CVD

EE 599 · Graduate power electronics · Nov 2026

              
Distributed buck converter with a lossy transmission line

              
I reproduced Röbenack & Gerbet (2024) in Python, derived the duty-to-current transfer functions from the telegrapher's equations, and extended them with an Lin ∥ Rp input element. Damping with Rp ≈ Z₀ cut the worst-case input ripple at the operating point from 16.3 A to 0.22 A.

IR-drop prediction with a fully convolutional U-Net

              
A PyTorch U-Net that predicts power-delivery-network voltage drop from current, PDN density and effective-distance maps. It was pretrained on synthetic layouts and fine-tuned on 10 real circuits. On unseen real designs it reached RMSE 0.54 mV and hotspot precision 0.73.

Signal integrity · Ansys HFSS research

                
Designing a via-fenced stripline for a package substrate

                
This is the structure I modeled most in HFSS: a trace buried in dielectric between two ground planes, with rows of stitching vias tying the planes together. The trace width sets impedance. The via pitch and fence spacing set how high in frequency the line stays clean before energy leaks into parasitic modes between the planes.

              

              

            


            

              

                
Power electronics · transmission lines

                
Why a 6 m cable breaks a buck converter, and how one resistor fixes it

                
Replacing the converter's inductor with a real coax line adds resonances in the PWM band. At 8.49 MHz the line rings and the input ripple reaches amps. A small inductor in series at the input, damped by a resistor near the line's Z₀, absorbs those reflections.
